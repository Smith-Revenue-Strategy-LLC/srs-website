Operator OS

Get out of the chat window.

A vendor-independent AI operating system for owners and independent operators. You stop re-explaining yourself to a chat box every morning, and you stop being stuck inside one company's product as better models ship.

Why a chat window is the wrong place to run your business

A chat interface is a great place to think and a terrible place to operate. It forgets. Every session starts from zero, so you spend the first ten minutes rebuilding context that you already built yesterday, and the quality of what you get back depends on how well you happened to explain yourself that morning.

It also hides your work. What you have built lives inside somebody's product, in their format, reachable only through their interface. You cannot diff it, you cannot version it, and you cannot hand it to anyone.

Operator OS moves the operating layer into plain files you own. Folders and markdown that any model, any agent, and any human can read. The AI becomes something you point at your system rather than something your system lives inside.

The two freedoms this buys you

Freedom from constant context infusion

Stop re-explaining yourself to a chat window every session just to get usable output. Your context, your standards, your voice, and your decisions live in files that load themselves. You show up and work.

Freedom from harness and model lock-in

New models ship constantly. When your operating layer is plain files, moving to a better one is a change of tool, not a migration. You are never stuck inside one provider's chat interface waiting for them to catch up.

It maintains itself

The system is built to correct and extend itself as you use it, on three engines:

  • Self-Healing. When it finds a fact in its own files that has gone stale or wrong, it fixes it in that same session. Reality wins over the document.
  • Self-Learning. Anything non-trivial it works out gets written down with its sources and its date, so the next session starts from what the last one learned instead of rediscovering it.
  • Self-Growing. A process you repeat becomes a saved procedure. Work that deserves a specialist gets one. The system gets more capable because you used it, not because you configured it.

What the first weeks look like

  • Homework first. A short setup sequence you complete on your own. It is deliberately a gate: it makes sure the system gets stood up around how you actually work rather than a generic template, and completing it unlocks the entry pricing.
  • Stand it up. Your context, your standards, and the two or three workflows that eat the most of your week get written into files.
  • Run it for a fortnight. You use it daily. The rough edges show themselves fast, and that is the point.
  • Tighten and extend. The workflows that earned their keep get deepened. The ones that did not get cut.

The first cohort is capped at roughly 15 to 20 people, and every one of them gets consults included at no charge to help stand the system up. Early participants also get beta access to the individual coaching features as they land, regular releases on what is actually changing in AI practice, and introductions to preferred cybersecurity and managed-services partners when they need them. Pricing for the entry offer is still being market-tested, which is part of why the first group is small.

An orchestrator is underway

In progress, not yet shipped.

We are building an AI orchestrator that will sit on top of the file structure and keep optimizing the workflows past their initial setup: noticing what you repeat, proposing where a step can come out, and tuning the structure as your work changes. It will ship to cohort participants as it becomes ready. It is named here because it is coming and it shapes the roadmap, not because it is available today.

The method underneath

The file structure is built on Interpretable Context Methodology, a published and citable method: Van Clief and McDermott, arXiv 2603.16021v2, 18 March 2026, MIT licensed. The core idea is that the folders and the markdown ARE the program, which is exactly what makes the result portable between models.

The way we teach the balance is our own frame, developed in practice here and not drawn from that paper: roughly 60 percent deterministic files, 30 percent orchestration rules, and 10 percent messy human prompting. The 60/30/10 split is what keeps a system legible to a human while still doing real work.
